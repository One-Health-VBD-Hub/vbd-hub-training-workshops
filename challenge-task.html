10 Challenge Task

This Challenge Task is your opportunity to put the skills you have learnt into practice, from searching for and retrieving data all the way to communicating your findings to a specific audience. There is no single correct answer, we are most interested in the decisions you make and why you made them.

10.1 Challenge Overview

You may work independently or in small groups. There are two key choices to make:

1. Choose your route:

  • Route A: Species distribution modelling - Your aim is to model where a vector could be found across Europe, now and under future climate, using occurrence and climate data.
  • Route B: Thermal performance curves - Your aim is to investigate a trait’s temperature response.

The instructions for each route are detailed below.

2. Choose your audience On Friday, you will have the chance to share your work in a brief presentation aimed at your choice of an academic, policy or public audience.

In your presentation, you might choose to include:

  • Your question, route and audience.
  • Your dataset, and the main decisions you made when checking and wrangling it.
  • Your hypothesis, finding, and any relevant uncertainty.
  • At least one key visualisation. Apply the CLEAR checklist so it is ready to present.
  • What your audience should do with this finding.
  • The dataset and software citations.

Before you plan your presentation, remember to consider who it is for and what information they need.

Feeling stuck?

If you are stuck at any point, there are several ways to find help:

  • Refer back to the content from the previous workshops.
  • Discuss it with your group or other participants. Other people might have the same questions as you!
  • Ask the demonstrators. They are there to help you.

If you cannot retrieve your data, ask a demonstrator for a saved copy of your dataset. You can continue with your analysis while the connection is resolved.

10.2 Route A: Species Distribution Modelling

Your aim is to model where a vector could be found across Europe, now and under future climate, using occurrence and climate data.

Choose one of these vectors:

  • Ixodes ricinus
  • Aedes albopictus
  • Culicoides obsoletus

As in the earlier workshop, rate your analysis against the Araujo et al. criteria as you go, and aim to improve its rating.

Find and check your observation data: Retrieve occurrence records for your chosen vector across Europe, limited to 10,000 records. Remember:

  1. ohvbd identifies your vector.
  2. geodata downloads only your vector’s records.
  3. ohvbd lists which GBIF datasets your records came from.

Check the records you retrieve, including their resolution, sources, and any location errors.

Find and check your environmental data: Retrieve current climate data for Europe. Check that its spatial resolution matches the scale of your occurrence data, and consider how accurate it is.

Tip:

Work in the same R project folder you used for the Species Distribution Modelling workshop to access your climate data from earlier.

Wrangle and form a hypothesis: Select the longitude and latitude of your records, and check for missing values. Before you build your model, write one testable hypothesis about which climate conditions shape where your vector is found, based on what you know about its biology.

Build your model: Fit a distribution model for your vector:

  • Check whether your climate variables are correlated.
  • What pseudo-absence strategy are you using, and do you agree with it?
  • Are the geographic and environmental extents of your model wide enough?

Evaluate your model: Plot the response curves and the predicted current distribution: - Where in space is your model under- or over-predicting occurrences? - Can you plot a ROC curve for your model?

Interpret and communicate: Explain what your model says about your hypothesis, and identify at least one source of uncertainty. Distinguish an observed pattern from a causal conclusion, and tailor the explanation to your chosen academic, policy or public audience. Include the dataset and software citations.

If time allows: Project your model under a future climate scenario, and map where your vector’s range is predicted to be gained or lost.

10.3 Route B: Thermal Performance Curves

Your aim is to investigate a trait’s temperature response with a dataset of your choice from:

Dataset to find through the Hub What you will analyse Question to explore
Aedes aegypti development rate — Yang et al. (2009), DOI 10.1017/S0950268809002040 Juvenile development rate, excluding the egg stage, in days⁻¹ Does development rate show a peak across the measured temperatures?
Anopheles gambiae juvenile survival — Bayoh and Lindsay (2003), DOI 10.1079/BER2003259 Survival of aquatic juveniles, in percent At which temperatures is survival highest, and how well does a symmetric curve describe the response?

Find and check your data. Search by species and identify the chosen trait and study. Retrieve it using ohvbd; dataset names are supplied here so that you can work out the retrieval yourself. Inspect the metadata, explain what each row represents and retain the source and curation citations.

Wrangle, visualise and form a hypothesis. Select the assay temperature and trait value, check units and missing values, and retain genuine zeros. Keep one consistent experimental group. Plot the data and write one testable hypothesis about the temperature response before fitting.

Analyse. Adapt the workshop’s rTPC workflow to fit a Gaussian curve. Use starting values and limits appropriate to your data, check convergence, and plot predictions within the observed range. Inspect the residuals and consider whether the symmetric shape is appropriate.

Survival option: the generic column name rate will contain a percentage in this dataset. Label your axis accordingly and constrain rmax to at most 100: set tpc_upper["rmax"] <- 100 before fitting. A least-squares curve fitted to percentages does not model the underlying numbers surviving and exposed. Discuss that limitation; we do not have those counts in the teaching table.

Interpret and communicate. Explain what your analysis says about your hypothesis. Prepare a clearly labelled data-and-fit plot, give the fitted peak with units, and identify at least one source of uncertainty. Distinguish an observed pattern from a causal conclusion, and tailor the explanation to your chosen academic, policy or public audience. Include the dataset and software citations.

If time allows: investigate how the fitted peak changes when you omit the hottest observations. Explain both the lost information and any change in the parameter limits.
